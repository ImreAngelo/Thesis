\chapter{Background}
\label{chp:background}

Anonymous communication aims to enable users to exchange messages over a network without revealing who is communicating with whom, thereby protecting both sender and receiver identities. Many such systems have been proposed and implemented over the years, such as Tor, I2P, and mixnets. Most of these designs depend on the \emph{threshold model} to provide anonymity, requiring at least some components of their infrastructure to behave honestly. In practice, this assumption is increasingly strained by large-scale surveillance, correlation attacks, and the growing centralization of internet infrastructure~\cite{sun:2015:RAPTOR}.

Systems with stronger anonymity guarantees based on \gls{mpc} exist. However, these suffer from poor scalability due to their high interactivity, making them impractical for large-scale networks. A \gls{dc-net}, for example, can provide anonymity even if there are no trusted components in the network infrastructure. Doing so requires every participant to interact with every other participant, for every round of messages sent.

Recently, a new line of research has proposed fundamentally different designs, aiming to offer strong anonymity even in the presence of compromised or adversarial components in the network actively attempting to break anonymity. The first such design was the \gls{niar}~\cite{cryptoeprint:2021/435}, which proposed using \gls{mcfe} to allow a single, untrusted router to shuffle or permute messages from a set of clients \emph{obliviously}, without learning anything about the permutation. This makes it infeasible for the router to link any of the messages to its sender.

\gls{niar} was a breakthrough in anonymous communication, not only showing the theoretical possibility of designing anonymous routers, but also that such routers can be \emph{non-interactive}: clients only send one message to the router and do not need to perform any additional interaction to achieve anonymity. Using a centralized (anonymous) router in this way yields similar benefits to existing \gls{mpc}-based systems, without needing any interactions between clients. However, there are a number of problems that makes \gls{niar} impractical, if not impossible, to implement in practice.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The cryptographic components required by NIAR are extremely computationally expensive or not known to be implementable in practice.
    \item Anonymity for recipients rests on \emph{indistinguishability obfuscation with sub-exponential security}, a primitive guaranteeing that no efficient adversary distinguishes the obfuscations of two functionally equivalent circuits with more than sub-exponentially small advantage. Whether it can be constructed practically is unknown.
    \item Reusing the same setup across multiple rounds makes messages from the same sender linkable, unless the expensive setup phase is rerun for every round.
\end{enumerate}

These problems were taken up by the \gls{spar}~\cite{cryptoeprint:2025/860}, which retains the anonymous-router functionality of \gls{niar} but rebuilds it on \gls{fhe}. A server able to compute on ciphertexts can place messages into a shuffled list without decrypting any of them, so the permutation exists only inside the computation and never in the server's view. Every primitive the construction requires is one that established encryption schemes already provide, recipient anonymity follows from an ordinary encryption layer rather than from obfuscation, and the permutation is redrawn from client randomness in every round. The price is a second round trip, since the decryption key is split across the clients and the shuffled batch cannot be opened without them.

The idea that a server might compute on data it is unable to read is as old as public-key cryptography itself. Rivest, Adleman and Dertouzos observed that RSA is multiplicative with respect to multiplication, and asked whether a \emph{fully} homomorphic scheme could exist~\cite{rivest:1978:databanks}. The question stayed open for three decades. Schemes were found that supported unbounded additions or a single multiplication, but never both without bound, as every candidate accumulated noise until its ciphertexts ceased to decrypt. Gentry settled the question in 2009 by evaluating a scheme's own decryption circuit homomorphically, refreshing the noise of a ciphertext instead of letting it grow, and obtained the first \gls{fhe} scheme~\cite{gentry:2009}.

\gls{fhe} is frequently called the holy grail of privacy-preserving computation, as it dissolves the usual trade-off between using a service and revealing data to it. For anonymous communication the appeal is more specific. An anonymous router is precisely a computation whose inputs and outputs the computing party must not learn, which is the problem \gls{fhe} was built to solve. Gentry's construction was far too slow to deploy, but the schemes that followed it have become fast enough that the question is no longer whether such a router can be described, but what it costs to run.

That question is open for \gls{spar}. The original paper establishes the construction and demonstrates it on a proof-of-concept built with TFHE, leaving its behaviour under a mature, exact-arithmetic scheme and its scaling in the number of participants unmeasured. This thesis closes that gap by implementing the protocol on BGV and evaluating it empirically.

\section{Contributions}
This thesis makes three contributions.

The first is a port of the TFHE-style external and internal products to the RNS variant of BGV. These products are what make long chains of encrypted binary multiplications affordable, but they are defined over a digit decomposition of ciphertext coefficients, which an RNS implementation never materializes. This construction is delivered as a library that extends OpenFHE~\cite{cryptoeprint:2022/915} and is usable independently from \gls{spar}. The construction is given in \autoref{chp:methodology}.

The second is a complete implementation of \gls{spar} on top of that library. And finally, the first empirical evaluation of the protocol beyond its original proof of concept. The implementation is described in \autoref{chp:methodology} and measured in \autoref{chp:results}.

\section{Structure of the Thesis}
Chapter \ref{chp:protocol} introduces the building blocks the protocol rests on, covering \gls{fhe} and the BGV scheme, residue number systems, gadget decomposition and the two RGSW products, before presenting \gls{spar} itself and the requirements it places on the scheme beneath it. Chapter \ref{chp:methodology} describes the implementation of both libraries and the experimental setup used to obtain the measurements. Chapter \ref{chp:results} reports the parameters, the noise growth and the benchmarks, and proposes a possible improvement to the protocol. Finally, \autoref{chp:conclusion} concludes the report and outlines the remaining work.
