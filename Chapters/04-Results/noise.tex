\section{Noise Analysis}
\label{sec:results:noise}
This section presents the noise growth of the implemented functions, reported throughout as the max-norm $\|\epsilon\|_\infty = \max_i\{\epsilon_i\}$. The absolute magnitude of the error is of little interest on its own; what matters is how many bits of the modulus it occupies. Every plot in this section is therefore semi-logarithmic with a base-2 $y$ axis, such that a vertical distance reads directly as a number of bits.  %% TODO: Rewrite

\subsection{Core Library}
We analyze the noise growth in the \gls{rgsw} products by measuring the noise after each iteration of the recurrence relation $x_i = x_{i-1} \cdot A_i$ where $\cdot$ represents the external or internal product. The initial $x_0 = \Call{Encrypt}{1}$ is an \gls{rlwe} in the external product $\boxdot$ and an \gls{rgsw} in the internal product $\boxtimes$. A fresh \gls{rgsw} $A_i = \Call{RGSW}{1}$ is supplied every iteration. Due to the asymmetry discussed in~\autoref{rem:extprod-asymmetry} making the noise additive instead of multiplicative, we expect to see linear noise growth with repeated applications of \gls{rgsw} products as long as the right operand remains fresh.

\subsubsection{External Product}
The measured growth is linear in the length of the chain, and the error gains only a few bits across the full chain at every value of $\ell$. Increasing $\ell$ shifts the whole series downwards without changing its slope.

\subsubsection{Internal product}
Again we see exactly what we expect; the growth is linear in the length of the chain and shifts downwards with $\ell$. The amount of noise added per product is substantially larger than for the external product.

Due to the RAM configuration of the testing environment and per-run time constraints (see \autoref{tab:bench:full}), we were unable to test $\eta > 8$. However, we are able to estimate that each user adds approximately $23$ bits of error under the parameters from \autoref{tab:bench:params}. Decryption fails once the error exceeds $\lfloor Q/2 \rfloor = 419$ bits. Under this estimate we are able to support $\eta = 9$ users, though this leaves only 3 bits of headroom for random variance. The number of users that can \emph{reliably} be supported is therefore $\eta = 8$. % Increasing $\ell$ or $k$ naturally increases the number of users supported, but significantly decreases performance.

Initially we suspected this number to be artificially low due to the large $t$. Since the $h \gets I_{i,k}\boxtimes\mathsf{notHasWritten}\boxtimes z_{i,d}$ assignment has only a single fresh ciphertext, $z_{i,d}$, a ``dirty'' ciphertext necessarily must appear on the right hand side in one of the internal products, breaking the noise asymmetry from \autoref{rem:extprod-asymmetry}. However, running the same test with $t=2^8$ decreased the noise by a negligible amount.

Testing without multi-party FHE, where every user share a single secret key, reveals the noise growth from the protocol itself is much smaller; to the point where a practically unbounded number of users (on the order of ${10^{41}}$) is supported. This confirms that the large noise in the multi-party setting comes from the noise flooding rather than the protocol itself. The flooding is intrinsic to distributed decryption, where it statistically hides each party's key share; switching to \texttt{FIXED\_NOISE\_MULTIPARTY}, whose flooding standard deviation is fixed and smaller, should support more users but would weaken that hiding guarantee and require a separate security analysis.
