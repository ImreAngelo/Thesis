\section{Noise Analysis}
\label{sec:results:noise}
This section presents the noise growth of the implemented functions, reported throughout as the max-norm $\|\epsilon\|_\infty = \max_i\{\epsilon_i\}$. The absolute magnitude of the error is of little interest on its own; what matters is how many bits of the modulus it occupies. Every plot in this section is therefore semi-logarithmic with a base-2 $y$ axis. %% TODO: Rewrite

\subsection{Core Library}
We analyze the noise growth in the \gls{rgsw} products by measuring the noise after each iteration of the recurrence relation $x_i = x_{i-1} \cdot A_i$ where $\cdot$ represents the external or internal product. The initial $x_0 = \Call{Encrypt}{1}$ is an \gls{rlwe} in the external product $\boxdot$ and an \gls{rgsw} in the internal product $\boxtimes$. A fresh \gls{rgsw} $A_i = \Call{RGSW}{1}$ is supplied every iteration. Due to the gadget property making the noise additive instead of multiplicative, and the asymmetry discussed in~\cref{rem:extprod-asymmetry}, we expect to see linear noise growth with repeated applications of \gls{rgsw} products as long as the right operand remains fresh.

\subsubsection{External Product}
The measured growth is linear in the length of the chain. After 300 iterations the maximum error has increased by 4 bits, by the end occupying 69 bits at the baseline $\ell = 2$. Increasing $\ell$ shifts the whole series downwards without changing its slope.

\subsubsection{Internal product}
Again we see exactly what we expect; the growth is linear in the length of the chain and shifts downwards with $\ell$. The amount of noise added per product is substantially larger than for the external product, taking up $116$ bits after 300 iterations at $\ell=2$.

We were unable to run the protocol at $\eta=16$ users without producing decryption failures. Decryption fails once the error exceeds $\lfloor Q/2 \rfloor = 419$ bits, and we can extrapolate that each user adds approximately $20.55$ bits of noise. On paper, then, the protocol supports $\eta = 9$ users, though this leaves only a few bits of room for random variance. The number of users that can \emph{reliably} be supported is therefore $\eta = 8$. Increasing the decomposition parameter improves the noise as expected, but also increases the runtime of the algorithm by the same factor.

Initially we suspected this number to be artificially low due to the large $t$, as BGV encodes the error in the most significant bits ($\epsilon = t\epsilon_{rlwe}$). Additionally, $h \gets I_{i,k}\boxtimes\mathsf{notHasWritten}\boxtimes z_{i,d}$ is created from a single fresh ciphertext, $z_{i,d}$, so a ``dirty'' ciphertext necessarily must appear on the right hand side in one of the internal products, breaking the freshness assumption in \cref{rem:extprod-asymmetry}. However, running the same test with $t=2^8$ decreased the noise by a negligible amount.

Testing without multi-party FHE, where every user share a single secret key, reveals the noise growth from the protocol itself is much smaller; to the point where a practically unbounded number of users (on the order of ${10^{41}}$) is supported. This confirms that the large noise in the multi-party setting comes from the noise flooding rather than the protocol itself. The flooding is intrinsic to distributed decryption, where it statistically hides each party's key share; switching to \texttt{FIXED\_NOISE\_MULTIPARTY}, whose flooding standard deviation is fixed and smaller, should support more users but would weaken that hiding guarantee and require a separate security analysis.
